Exactly right — you've named the core problem. GDP is a monetary measure of output; it says nothing about distribution, sustainability, or lived experience. That's precisely why we need alternatives.

Now let me frame this for the exam. This topic sits at the intersection of macroeconomic measurement (Unit 3.1) and development economics (Unit 4.8). The examiners test it by asking you to:

- Define alternative measures (HDI, Gross National Happiness, Happy Planet Index, OECD Better Life)
- Explain why each one captures something GDP misses
- Evaluate whether any single alternative is "better" than GDP — spoiler: it depends on what you're measuring

The most common question format is "To what extent does [alternative measure] provide a better measure of national well-being than GDP?" That's a classic AO3 evaluate question, and it appears in both Paper 1(b) and Paper 2.

Here's your first exam-style question — this is Paper 1, Section B, 15 marks:

**To what extent does the Human Development Index (HDI) provide a better measure of national well-being than Gross Domestic Product (GDP)?**

## Turn 4 — Student submits mediocre 400-word answer

The Human Development Index (HDI) was created by the United Nations as a broader measure of national well-being than GDP. It combines three things: health (measured by life expectancy), education (measured by years of schooling), and standard of living (measured by GNI per capita). Each country gets a score between 0 and 1, where higher means more developed.

HDI is better than GDP in several ways. First, it captures dimensions GDP ignores. A country could have high GDP per capita but very low life expectancy, suggesting the wealth is not being used to improve health. HDI would flag this gap clearly. Second, HDI is more useful for comparing development across countries because it includes social outcomes. Norway and Qatar have similar GDP per capita but very different HDI scores. Third, HDI gives policymakers a clearer picture of where to direct development spending — whether on health, education, or income generation.

However, HDI has limitations. It does not measure inequality within countries, so a country with high inequality could still have a strong HDI. It does not measure environmental sustainability, which is a major weakness given climate change. It also does not capture subjective well-being or happiness — measures like Bhutan's Gross National Happiness include these dimensions.

GDP also has strengths that HDI lacks. GDP is updated quarterly while HDI is updated annually, so GDP is more useful for short-term economic decisions. GDP captures economic activity directly, which is essential for tracking growth, unemployment, and inflation.

In conclusion, HDI provides a better measure of national well-being than GDP in most contexts because it captures health and education alongside income. However, neither measure is complete on its own. The best approach is to use HDI for development comparisons and policy decisions, while using GDP for short-term economic monitoring.

## Turn 5 — Mia marks with band threshold + structural framework + redo

Good start — you've structured a clear argument with definition, multiple perspectives, and a conclusion. Let me mark this against the IBO 15-mark band.

**What you've done well:**

- Defined HDI clearly (three components, 0–1 scale)
- Explained why HDI captures dimensions GDP misses (health and education example)
- Acknowledged HDI limitations (inequality, environment, subjective well-being)
- Included a real-world comparative example (Norway vs Qatar)
- Reached a qualified conclusion

**Where it falls short of 13–15 (full mark band):**

**1. Evaluation lacks depth and balance.** You've listed strengths of HDI and weaknesses of HDI, but you haven't genuinely weighed them. An evaluative answer at 13–15 level needs to ask: *Under what conditions is HDI better, and under what conditions is GDP better?* You touched on this ("GDP is more useful for short-term decisions") but didn't develop it into a framework. The examiner wants to see you recognise that both measures serve different purposes — not just conclude HDI is "better in most contexts."

**2. Missing a second alternative measure.** A 13–15 answer compares GDP to at least two alternatives, not just HDI. You mentioned Gross National Happiness and environmental sustainability but didn't develop them. The OECD Better Life Index or Happy Planet Index would strengthen your evaluation by showing HDI itself has limitations that other measures address.

**3. Weak criterion for "better."** You didn't define what "better" means. *Better for what? Better for whom?* The question asks about "national well-being" — you should have clarified early: well-being for development policy? For economic stability? For citizen satisfaction? Different measures suit different purposes.

**Here's what 13–15 looks like:**

Define well-being → argue HDI captures dimensions GDP misses (health/education) with example → acknowledge HDI ignores inequality and environment → introduce a second alternative (e.g., Happy Planet Index captures sustainability, OECD Better Life captures subjective dimensions) → evaluate: GDP best for short-term macro management, HDI best for development comparisons, but no single measure is complete because well-being is multidimensional → conclude with a conditional statement: *"HDI is superior to GDP for assessing human development, but for economic stability monitoring, GDP remains essential. A composite approach using HDI for policy goals and GDP for cyclical tracking is most effective."*

Redo this answer using that framework. Aim for the same length, but add a second alternative measure and rebuild your evaluation to show conditional superiority rather than absolute ranking.
