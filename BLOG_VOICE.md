# BLOG_VOICE.md
The house voice for every Gradd blog article. This is the outward face of the Gradd teaching method (see TEACHING_PRINCIPLES.md) — the blog corrects a misconception the same way Mia does. Every article is written in this voice from the first draft. Self-check each draft against the rules below before it leaves Claude Code.

## The voice in one line
A sharp teacher correcting a confused student — not a textbook, not a model explaining a syllabus.

## Preferred rhythm
Wrong idea → correction → one sharp example → the rule → exam consequence (mark-cost) → Mia bridge. A spine, not a template — vary it so articles don't read identically, but every article hits these beats.

## Do
1. **Open on the confusion, never the definition.** First lines name the wrong thing the reader probably believes. No "In economics, demand is…" warm-ups.
2. **Talk to "you."** Address the reader directly. Correct them like a patient teacher.
3. **One sharp example beats three vague ones.** Pick a single concrete case and stay with it.
4. **Short sentence after a hard idea.** Let the difficult point land before moving on.
5. **Make them feel they caught something.** End so the reader feels they spotted the trap — not that they were lectured.
6. **State the rule plainly, once.** The fix is one clean sentence the reader can carry into the exam.
7. **Vary paragraph length.** Some one line. Some four. Never a uniform wall.
8. **Name the mark-cost.** Show how the mistake breaks the diagram, calculation, chain of reasoning, or evaluation. Every article ties the error to lost marks — that's what creates urgency.
9. **Teach the thinking first, then the phrase.** Use exam/command-term language only after the idea is clear. The reader understands the model, then gets the sentence to write.
10. **Make the correction reusable.** The reader leaves with one sentence they can use verbatim in an exam answer.
11. **Firm, not superior.** The reader should feel they caught the trap, not that they were mocked. "That sentence feels right — but it's the wrong model," never "you're wrong, fix it."

## Don't
1. **No connective filler:** "it's worth noting," "that said," "draws a hard line," "it's important to remember," "let's dive in."
2. **No explaining that you're explaining:** "In this article we'll look at…"
3. **No tidy tricolons or over-balanced sentences** as a default rhythm — they're the clearest AI tell.
4. **No lazy hedging:** avoid "generally," "often," "tends to" — unless the guide, mark scheme, or the real-world model genuinely requires the caveat. Accuracy wins over punchiness.
5. **No syllabus-narrator register:** "The IB curriculum distinguishes between…" Write what a teacher says, not what a spec says.
6. **Don't bury the rule.** If the reader can't find the one-sentence fix on a skim, rewrite.

## The product bridge (every article)
Don't say "Mia teaches this." Say Mia spots the faulty model under the answer, names it, and makes you redraw it — before you've practised the wrong thing twenty times. The diagnosis is the moat; show it.

## The non-negotiable
Voice edits never override content accuracy. Every economics/BM claim still passes the adversarial guide-check before publish. Sounding human is not a licence to drift from the official guide.

## The filter for killing AI copy
If it reads like a curriculum narrator, rewrite it. If it reads like a sharp teacher correcting a real mistake, keep it.
