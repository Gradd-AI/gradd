Professional skills are not a bonus.

They are 20 marks.

Section A is worth 50 marks. Ten of those marks are for professional skills. Each Section B question is worth 25 marks, with 5 professional skills marks attached to each one.

That means 20 marks out of 100 sit in a column many candidates barely practise.

You can know the technical content and still leave a fifth of the paper half-empty.

Here's what that column actually rewards.

## Professional skills are not a separate essay

This is the first trap.

Students look for a separate professional-skills paragraph.

There usually isn't one.

The marks come from how you write the technical answer: how you structure it, how you use the scenario, how you challenge the information, and whether your recommendation sounds like something a real manager could act on.

Same technical point.

Different professional skill.

That difference matters.

### 1. Communication

Communication is not "nice writing".

It is whether the answer is usable by the person named in the requirement.

If the requirement asks for a report to the board, write like you're briefing the board. Use headings. Lead with the issue. Keep the register professional. Make the recommendation clear.

Weak communication:

> The balanced scorecard has four perspectives and can be used to assess financial and non-financial performance.

Better communication:

> **Customer service is the immediate performance concern.** Complaint volumes have risen by 18%, yet the current report gives the board no measure of response time or resolution quality. The report should add one service-quality measure before the next board review.

The second answer is easier to mark.

More importantly, it is easier for the board to use.

### 2. Analysis and evaluation

Analysis is not repeating a number.

Evaluation is not adding the word "therefore".

APM wants developed points that explain what the information means, why it matters, and how much weight to put on it.

Weak analysis:

> Gross margin fell from 42% to 38%, which is bad.

Better analysis:

> Gross margin fell from 42% to 38%, despite revenue growth. This suggests the growth may have been won through discounting or higher service costs, so the revenue increase should not be treated as a clear sign of improved performance.

Now the number has been interpreted.

The point has a consequence.

That is analysis.

### 3. Scepticism

Scepticism means you do not accept every number, forecast, consultant claim or management assertion at face value.

APM scenarios often hand you information that looks useful but needs challenging.

Weak scepticism:

> The consultant forecasts a 15% cost saving, so the project is beneficial.

Better scepticism:

> The 15% cost saving is only a forecast from the consultant selling the system, so it may be optimistic. The board should ask for the assumptions behind the saving and compare them with actual implementation costs from similar projects.

That is scepticism.

You have challenged the source, the assumption, and the reliability of the claim.

### 4. Commercial acumen

Commercial acumen means your answer survives contact with the real business.

It considers cost, disruption, timing, capacity, behaviour and implementation.

Weak commercial acumen:

> The company should introduce a new performance dashboard.

Better commercial acumen:

> A new dashboard may help, but only if the business first agrees which measures managers will actually use. Otherwise it risks becoming another reporting layer, adding cost and delay without changing decisions.

That is commercial.

It recognises that a technically attractive answer can still fail in practice.

## Why most candidates do not practise these marks

Professional skills feel invisible.

There is no formula to memorise.

No model to draw.

No neat definition to recite.

So candidates assume the skills will appear naturally if the technical answer is good.

They won't.

A technically correct answer can still lose professional-skills marks if it is a wall of text, accepts every figure blindly, makes no judgement, or ends with a recommendation no manager could implement.

The technical answer gets you into the mark scheme.

Professional skills decide how much of it you actually convert.

## How to practise them deliberately

Before you write, ask which professional skill the requirement is pulling hardest.

> "Prepare a report advising the board…"

That is communication and commercial acumen.

So you need a clear structure, a board-level tone, and a recommendation the board could act on.

> "Evaluate the consultant's proposal…"

That is analysis, evaluation and scepticism.

So you need to weigh the proposal, challenge the assumptions, and decide whether it is worth doing.

> "Assess whether the current performance measures are appropriate…"

That is analysis and evaluation.

So you need to judge relevance, not describe the measures.

Then check the answer before you move on.

Ask four questions:

1. Is it structured for the person named in the requirement?
2. Have I explained what the numbers mean, not just repeated them?
3. Have I challenged any weak data, claim or assumption?
4. Have I landed on something commercially usable?

If not, the marks were there and you left them.
